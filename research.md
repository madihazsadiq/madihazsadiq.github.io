  - title: "Imperial Agents Return: the case of Manchuria"
    description: My thesis examines the postwar experience of Japanese civilians repatriated from Manchuria after the collapse of the Japanese empire in 1945. Between 1945 and 1948, roughly 6.6 million Japanese were repatriated from across Asia — one of the largest mass population transfers of the twentieth century, yet still understudied. Unlike most migration cases, repatriates returned to a society sharing their language, ethnicity, and nationality, making this a useful test of whether cultural sameness is enough for smooth reintegration, or whether other mechanisms of exclusion operate independently of it. I develop "status dislocation" as a framework to explain the abrupt social, symbolic, and material decline that follows a major structural shock like regime change — linking macro-level triggers, meso-level processes like censorship, and micro-level outcomes like social stigma. The framework also considers how local context shapes this decline, namely, communities with strong civic autonomy appear to buffer stigma, while those closely tied to national identity appear to amplify it. Empirically, I draw on a 1956 government survey of repatriates' postwar lives and 73 interviews with returnees in Nagano Prefecture, using post-return geographic mobility as an indicator of status dislocation.
    category: PhD Thesis
    status: "Expected completion: Autumn 2026"
  - title: "Between Frontlines and Home: the Effect of Returning Soldiers on Public Opinion in Norway, <i>with Juliette Saetre</i>"
    description: "Abstract: How does a nation’s support for one side of a conflict shift dramatically to the other? This paper examines a rapid shift in Norwegian public opinion between 1978 and 1980, from strong pro-Israel sentiment to widespread criticism of Israel’s actions in the occupied territories. We propose a bottom-up explanation of opinion change: the diffusion of counter-narrative information through trusted in-group members who—by virtue of their mobility—become informationally distinct from their peers. Specifically, we focus on Norwegian UNIFIL peacekeepers returning from Lebanon after 1978 who, through their direct exposure to the conflict and continued embeddedness in local communities, came to serve as informational brokers. Combining cross-sectional data on public opinion with original survey data on former veterans, we leverage geographic variation in returnee intensity to show that counties receiving more returnees experienced sharper declines in pro-Israel sentiment. The findings demonstrate how short-term mobility can drive attitudinal change by enabling trusted in-group members to introduce new information into otherwise homogeneous environments."

  - title: "From Protection to Inclusion: Shifts and gendered implications of forced migration policy, <i>with Sandra Pertek & Maëlle Noir</i>"
    description: "Abstract: This paper examines the gendered implications of a critical yet underexplored shift in global refugee governance and policy rhetoric: the move from protection to inclusion in Global South contexts, through comparative case studies of Uganda and Pakistan. It interrogates whether the rhetoric of inclusion truly enhances gender-sensitive refugee governance or risks reproducing protection gaps—legitimising containment and externalising responsibility to Global South states. Advancing gendered refugee scholarship, the paper develops a hybrid analytical framework—drawing on the Sphere Project Protection Principles and the UK Home Office Indicators of Integration—to assess gender sensitivity across social policy domains. A gendered analysis reveals how policy design and implementation—even when well-intentioned—can reproduce inequalities and limit transformative potential. The comparative findings demonstrate that inclusion is not inherently emancipatory and must be critically examined within context. While inclusion is often framed as empowering and participatory, the analysis shows that it can essentialise gender by equating it with “women and girls” and reinforcing traditional roles (e.g., women as caregivers or victims), thereby neglecting intersectional vulnerabilities across work, education, and political participation. The paper calls for a gendered lens that moves beyond binary categories and victimhood narratives, engaging more deeply with intersectionality, agency, and structural inequalities in refugee governance."
